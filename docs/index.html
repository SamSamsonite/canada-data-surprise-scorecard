Canada Data Surprise Scorecard

Every month, Statistics Canada tells you how the economy did — three times, and not always the same thing. The forecast turns out to be the reassuring part: the advance estimate misses the official figure by 0.11 percentage points on average, and beats a rule as simple as repeating last month's number by 52%. The unsettling part is what happens afterwards. The published figure moves by 0.15 points on average once it is revised — more than the advance estimate was ever wrong by — and it moves up more often than not, by an average of 0.07 points. On average, the number everyone reacts to on release day has understated how the economy actually did — though, as the revisions section explains, that lean rests heavily on the post-pandemic recovery months and is suggestive rather than settled.

How a GDP number gets published

The same month's growth is reported three separate times, and the differences between them are what this page measures.

  1. The advance estimate. Roughly a month before the official figure, StatCan publishes a preliminary number based on incomplete data. It appears inside the previous month's release.
  2. The first print. The first official figure. This is the one that makes headlines and that markets trade on.
  3. The revisions. As fuller source data arrives, the figure is quietly corrected — for years afterwards.

The part nobody watches: what happens after the headline

Revisions to the first published figure

Revisions move the published figure by 0.15 percentage points on average, and they lean upward: the average revision is +0.07 points, which on the full sample is large enough relative to its spread to be unlikely to be chance (t = +2.55).

What this shows
For each month, how far today's revised GDP figure sits from the number that was first published.
Why it matters
Markets, newsrooms and policymakers react to the first print. If that number moves materially afterwards, decisions were made on a figure that no longer exists.

Read this with the finding above: That lean is not evenly spread across the sample. Dropping the earliest 12 months — the post-pandemic recovery, when GDP was moving fast and source data was unusually incomplete — the average revision is still upward at +0.04 points, but it is no longer large enough relative to its spread to rule out chance (t = +1.75, n = 47). The direction is consistent; the strength of the evidence for it is not. Treat the upward lean as suggestive rather than settled.

Does the advance estimate beat just guessing?

The advance estimate does beat both naive rules, cutting the average miss by 52% against simply repeating last month's number. Both naive rules are computed only from figures that had actually been published at the time, so they are not given information from the future.

What this shows
The advance estimate's average miss, next to two rules that use no judgement at all: repeat last month's growth, or average the last three months.
Why it matters
A forecast is only worth producing if it beats a rule anyone could apply in their head. This is the benchmark every other number on the page is measured against.

What the words mean

Advance estimate
A preliminary figure Statistics Canada publishes for the following month, inside each monthly GDP release, based on incomplete source data. It is an official, on-the-record forecast from the agency itself.
First print
The first official figure for a month. This is the number that appears in headlines and that markets react to on release day.
Revision
A later change to an already-published figure, as more complete source data arrives. Revisions continue for years after the first print.
Percentage point (pp)
The unit for a gap between two percentages. If the advance estimate said growth of 0.2% and the official figure was 0.5%, the miss is 0.3 percentage points.
Naive benchmark
A deliberately simple rule — here, repeating last month's figure, or averaging the last three. Any forecast that cannot beat it is not adding information.

How this was built, and what it cannot tell you

Every number above comes from two free, openly-licensed sources: the Statistics Canada Web Data Service (table 36-10-0434-01, monthly GDP by industry, seasonally adjusted, chained 2017 dollars) and the Bank of Canada Valet API. No paid or licensed forecast data is used anywhere.

The advance estimates and first prints are not available from the API at all. Statistics Canada's data service returns only current, fully-revised values, so it cannot tell you what a figure said on the day it was released. Those numbers were recovered from The Daily, StatCan's official release bulletin, which states both figures on the record in every monthly GDP release.

  • Rounding limits the precision. StatCan publishes these changes to one decimal place, so revisions smaller than 0.1 percentage points are invisible to this method.
  • The sample is short. 49 months is enough to describe what happened but not enough to settle whether a small average bias is real. Where a figure is too small to distinguish from zero, the page says so.
  • Recent months are under-revised. The most recent months have had less time to be revised than older ones, which mechanically shrinks their revisions.
  • CPI and the Labour Force Survey are not scored here. Neither publishes an advance estimate, so the central comparison on this page does not exist for them. Adding them would have meant a different, weaker chart rather than the same one with more data.
